Polynomial Identity Testing - When Randomness Checks Your Algebra

Aug 2021
9 min read

Helpful context:

  • Randomized Algorithms - When a Coin Flip Beats Careful Thinking
  • Abstract Algebra: Rings & Fields - When Numbers Break Their Own Rules

Are two polynomials $f$ and $g$ the same? The obvious method is to expand both and compare coefficients, and it can be hopeless. A small arithmetic circuit for a determinant, or a product of many linear factors, can stand for a polynomial with exponentially many terms.

The algebraic shortcut: plug in a random point. If $f(r) \neq g(r)$, they’re definitely different. If they agree, they’re probably identical, and the chance of being fooled is at most the degree divided by the size of the set you sampled from.

The idea that a nonzero polynomial can’t vanish at too many points runs through theoretical computer science: verifying matrix products, perfect matchings, primality, interactive and zero-knowledge proofs, error-correcting codes. And it’s not fully understood. Making polynomial identity testing deterministic is tied to proving circuit lower bounds, among the hardest open problems in complexity.

The Problem

Given an arithmetic circuit computing a polynomial $p(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ over a field $F$, is $p$ identically zero, with every coefficient zero, rather than merely zero at some inputs? (Testing $f = g$ is testing whether $f - g$ is zero.)

  • In the blackbox model you can only evaluate $p$ at points you choose.
  • In the whitebox model you can also inspect the circuit.

Expanding into monomials is correct but can explode: a degree-$d$ polynomial in $n$ variables can have up to $\binom{n+d}{d}$ of them, and the permanent of an $n \times n$ matrix has $n!$.

The Schwartz–Zippel Lemma

Lemma (DeMillo–Lipton 1977, Zippel 1979, Schwartz 1980). Let $p$ be a nonzero polynomial of total degree at most $d$ over a field $F$, and $S$ a finite subset of $F$. Choosing each $r_i$ independently and uniformly from $S$, $$\Pr[p(r_1, \ldots, r_n) = 0] \leq \frac{d}{|S|}$$

For one variable it’s the familiar fact that a nonzero degree-$d$ polynomial has at most $d$ roots. The lemma says the same holds, in probability, however many variables there are.

Proof by induction on the number of variables

Proof by induction on $n$.

Base case $n = 1$. A nonzero univariate polynomial of degree at most $d$ has at most $d$ roots in any field. Choosing $r_1$ uniformly at random from $S$, the probability of hitting a root is at most $d / |S|$.

Inductive step. Assume the lemma holds for polynomials in $n - 1$ variables. Write $p$ as a polynomial in $x_1$ with coefficients in $F[x_2, \ldots, x_n]$:

$$p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{i=0}^{d} x_1^i \cdot q_i(x_2, \ldots, x_n)$$

Let $k$ be the largest index such that $q_k$ is not the zero polynomial. Such a $k$ exists because $p$ is nonzero. Note that $q_k$ has total degree at most $d - k$.

Fix independent uniform random choices $r_2, \ldots, r_n$ from $S$. There are two cases.

Case 1: $q_k(r_2, \ldots, r_n) = 0$. By the inductive hypothesis applied to $q_k$ (a nonzero polynomial in $n-1$ variables of degree at most $d - k$):

$$\Pr[q_k(r_2, \ldots, r_n) = 0] \leq \frac{d - k}{|S|}$$

Case 2: $q_k(r_2, \ldots, r_n) \neq 0$. Now $p(x_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n)$ is a nonzero univariate polynomial in $x_1$ of degree exactly $k$. Choosing $r_1$ uniformly from $S$:

$$\Pr[p(r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n) = 0 \mid q_k(r_2, \ldots, r_n) \neq 0] \leq \frac{k}{|S|}$$

Combining via the law of total probability:

$$\Pr[p(r_1, \ldots, r_n) = 0] \leq \Pr[q_k(r_2, \ldots, r_n) = 0] + \Pr[p = 0 \mid q_k \neq 0] \leq \frac{d-k}{|S|} + \frac{k}{|S|} = \frac{d}{|S|}$$

This completes the induction.

The test. Pick $S$ with $|S| = 2d$, evaluate the circuit at a random point, and report “zero” only if the result is 0. A nonzero polynomial fools it with probability at most $1/2$; with $k$ independent tries, at most $2^{-k}$. Each try is one circuit evaluation.

Freivalds' Algorithm: Checking a Matrix Product

Someone hands you $n \times n$ matrices $A$, $B$ and $C$ and claims $AB = C$. Recomputing $AB$ costs $O(n^3)$, or $O(n^{2.37})$ with the fastest known methods. Freivalds' algorithm (1979) checks in $O(n^2)$: pick a random 0/1 vector $r$, and compare $A(Br)$ with $Cr$. Each side is just two matrix-vector products.

If $AB = C$, the two sides always match. If not, they differ with probability at least 1/2, and $k$ rounds push the chance of missing the error below $2^{-k}$: $O(n^2 \log n)$ for a near-certain answer. It’s Schwartz–Zippel with degree 1 and a two-element sample set.

Why a wrong product is caught at least half the time

Error probability when $AB \neq C$. Let $D = AB - C$. Since $AB \neq C$, $D$ is a nonzero matrix. We need to bound $\Pr[Dr = 0]$ when $r$ is uniform over $\{0, 1\}^n$.

Since $D \neq 0$, there exists a row $i$ and a column $j$ such that $D_{ij} \neq 0$. The $i$-th component of $Dr$ is:

$$(Dr)_i = \sum_{k=1}^{n} D_{ik} r_k = D_{ij} r_j + \sum_{k \neq j} D_{ik} r_k$$

View this as a linear polynomial in $r_j$ alone, with all other $r_k$ fixed. The coefficient of $r_j$ is $D_{ij} \neq 0$. Over the domain $\{0, 1\}$ for $r_j$, a nonzero degree-1 polynomial has at most 1 root, so:

$$\Pr[(Dr)_i = 0 \mid r_1, \ldots, r_{j-1}, r_{j+1}, \ldots, r_n] \leq \frac{1}{2}$$

by Schwartz-Zippel with $d = 1$, $|S| = 2$. Since this holds for every fixed choice of the other coordinates:

$$\Pr[Dr = 0] \leq \Pr[(Dr)_i = 0] \leq \frac{1}{2}$$

The Isolation Lemma

Many problems - shortest paths, spanning trees, matchings - are easy one step at a time but hard to parallelize, because there may be many optimal answers and parallel processors can’t agree on which one to build. Mulmuley, Vazirani and Vazirani (1987) fixed this by making the optimum unique, at random.

Isolation Lemma. Give each of $n$ elements an independent random weight from $\{1, \ldots, 2n\}$. For any family of subsets, with probability at least $1/2$ there is exactly one subset of minimum total weight.

Why. For an element $i$, let $W_i^+$ be the lightest weight of a set containing $i$, and $W_i^-$ the lightest weight of a set without it. The minimum fails to be unique only if some $i$ is ambiguous: $W_i^+ = W_i^-$, with a lightest set containing $i$ and another avoiding it. Fix every other weight. Then $W_i^-$ doesn’t depend on $w_i$, while $W_i^+$ rises by exactly 1 for every 1 added to $w_i$, so they’re equal for at most one of the $2n$ possible values of $w_i$. Hence $i$ is ambiguous with probability at most $\frac{1}{2n}$, and a union bound over all $n$ elements gives at most $\frac{1}{2}$.

Perfect matching in parallel. For a bipartite graph with an $n \times n$ vertex split, give each edge a random weight $w_{ij}$ and build the weighted Edmonds matrix: $T_{ij} = 2^{w_{ij}}$ where there’s an edge, 0 elsewhere. Each perfect matching $M$ contributes $\pm 2^{w(M)}$ to $\det T$. If the minimum-weight matching is unique, with weight $w^*$, every other term is a higher power of 2, so the highest power of 2 dividing $\det T$ is exactly $2^{w^*}$: the determinant is nonzero. Determinants can be computed in polylogarithmic parallel time, so this puts bipartite perfect matching in RNC, a landmark result of the 1980s. (MVV extended it to general graphs using the Tutte matrix.)

Reed–Solomon Codes

The same fact about roots gives one of the most widely used error-correcting codes. Encode a message $(m_0, \ldots, m_{k-1})$ as the polynomial $p(x) = m_0 + m_1 x + \cdots + m_{k-1} x^{k-1}$, and transmit its values at $n$ fixed points: $\big(p(\alpha_1), \ldots, p(\alpha_n)\big)$.

Two different messages give different polynomials, whose difference has degree at most $k - 1$ and so at most $k - 1$ roots. So their codewords agree in at most $k - 1$ places and differ in at least $n - k + 1$. That meets the Singleton bound exactly, which makes Reed–Solomon codes maximum distance separable: no linear code of the same size does better. They protect CDs, QR codes, deep-space links and RAID storage.

The details

Reed-Solomon codes. Fix a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ and $n$ distinct evaluation points $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n \in \mathbb{F}_q$. A Reed-Solomon code of dimension $k$ encodes a message $(m_0, \ldots, m_{k-1}) \in \mathbb{F}_q^k$ as the evaluation vector:

$$\text{RS}(m) = (p(\alpha_1), p(\alpha_2), \ldots, p(\alpha_n))$$

where $p(x) = m_0 + m_1 x + \cdots + m_{k-1} x^{k-1}$ is a polynomial of degree at most $k - 1$.

Minimum distance. Two distinct codewords $\text{RS}(m)$ and $\text{RS}(m')$ correspond to distinct polynomials $p$ and $p'$, so $p - p'$ is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most $k - 1$. By the univariate case of Schwartz-Zippel (or just the fundamental theorem of algebra), $p - p'$ has at most $k - 1$ roots. So $p$ and $p'$ agree on at most $k - 1$ of the $n$ evaluation points.

Therefore, $\text{RS}(m)$ and $\text{RS}(m')$ differ in at least $n - (k-1) = n - k + 1$ positions. The minimum distance is:

$$d_{\min} = n - k + 1$$

This achieves the Singleton bound $d \leq n - k + 1$, making Reed-Solomon codes maximum distance separable (MDS) - optimal among all linear codes of the same rate.

The Schwartz-Zippel perspective: the probability that a uniformly random evaluation point fails to distinguish two distinct degree-$(k-1)$ polynomials is at most $(k-1)/q$. The MDS property is exactly this bound stated as a minimum distance.

Summary

Algorithm Question Randomness Error Cost
Schwartz–Zippel test Is $p \equiv 0$? A random point from $S$ $\leq d / \lvert S \rvert$ per try One circuit evaluation per try
Freivalds Is $AB = C$? A random 0/1 vector $\leq 1/2$ per try $O(n^2)$ per try
Isolation lemma Make the optimum unique Random weights in $\{1, \ldots, 2n\}$ $\leq 1/2$ failure Enables RNC matching
Reed–Solomon How far apart are codewords? None: a structural consequence Distance exactly $n - k + 1$ Optimal (MDS)

The same engine drives all four: a nonzero polynomial has few roots compared with the size of the field, and that turns hard combinatorial questions into easy probabilistic ones.

Derandomizing PIT, finding a deterministic polynomial-time test, is a central open problem. Impagliazzo and Wigderson showed that strong enough circuit lower bounds would give BPP = P, derandomizing it. In the other direction, Kabanets and Impagliazzo showed that derandomizing PIT would itself imply circuit lower bounds. The two problems rise or fall together.
