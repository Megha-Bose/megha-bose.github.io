Complexity Classes - Sorting Problems by How Hard They Are

Aug 2021
6 min read

Helpful context:

  • Space Complexity - When Memory, Not Time, Is the Bottleneck
  • P, NP, and the Hardest Problems - The Question That Keeps Theorists Up at Night

P, NP, co-NP, BPP, ZPP, RP, #P, PSPACE, EXPTIME, PH, IP, PPAD: computer scientists have catalogued hundreds of complexity classes. It isn’t collecting for its own sake. Each one captures a different kind of hardness: what happens when you add randomness, alternation, counting, or a conversation. Knowing where a problem sits tells you which tools fit, and which limits are fundamental.

A Map of the Territory

A class is defined by a resource bound (time, space, randomness, number of solutions) and a kind of machine (deterministic, nondeterministic, probabilistic, alternating). The backbone:

$$\mathbf{L} \subseteq \mathbf{NL} \subseteq \mathbf{P} \subseteq \mathbf{NP} \subseteq \mathbf{PSPACE} \subseteq \mathbf{EXPTIME}$$

Every inclusion is believed to be strict. Only the far ends are proven apart: $\mathbf{NL} \neq \mathbf{PSPACE}$ by the space hierarchy theorem, and $\mathbf{P} \neq \mathbf{EXPTIME}$ by the time hierarchy theorem. Everything in between is open.

The Polynomial Hierarchy

NP guesses one witness and checks it. What if checking the witness itself needs a guess? “Is there an $x$ such that for every $y$, there’s a $z$ with…": nested, alternating quantifiers. That’s the polynomial hierarchy. With $\Sigma_0^P = \mathbf{P}$:

$$\Sigma_k^P = \mathbf{NP}^{\Sigma_{k-1}^P}, \qquad \Pi_k^P = \text{co-}\Sigma_k^P, \qquad \mathbf{PH} = \bigcup_k \Sigma_k^P$$

Here $\mathbf{NP}^{\mathcal{C}}$ means NP with a free oracle for anything in class $\mathcal{C}$. So $\Sigma_1^P = \mathbf{NP}$, and $\Sigma_2^P = \mathbf{NP}^{\mathbf{NP}}$: guess something, then ask an NP oracle about it.

PH sits inside PSPACE. If any two levels coincide, the whole hierarchy collapses to that level, and P = NP would collapse it all the way down to P. Most theorists believe PH is infinite, which would imply P ≠ NP.

Randomness: BPP, RP, ZPP

Give the machine fair coins.

  • BPP: a randomized polynomial-time algorithm that’s right with probability at least 2/3 on every input. The 2/3 isn’t special: repeat $k$ times and take the majority vote, and the error drops exponentially in $k$.
  • RP: one-sided error. Never wrong when the answer is no; right at least half the time when it’s yes. co-RP is the mirror image.
  • ZPP = RP ∩ co-RP: never wrong, with polynomial expected running time. These are Las Vegas algorithms; Randomized Algorithms has examples.

$\mathbf{P} \subseteq \mathbf{ZPP} \subseteq \mathbf{RP} \subseteq \mathbf{BPP}$, and $\mathbf{RP} \subseteq \mathbf{NP}$. BPP even fits inside the second level of the hierarchy.

The big conjecture is BPP = P: randomness doesn’t really add power for yes/no questions. It follows from plausible circuit lower bounds, via Nisan and Wigderson’s pseudorandom generators, and many famous randomized algorithms have since been derandomized. Primality testing had fast randomized algorithms for decades before the AKS algorithm put it in P in 2002.

Counting: #P

NP asks whether a solution exists. #P asks how many there are: count the accepting paths of a nondeterministic polynomial-time machine. #SAT counts satisfying assignments; #MATCH counts perfect matchings in a bipartite graph.

Valiant’s theorem (1979): #SAT is #P-complete, and so is #MATCH, even though deciding whether a perfect matching exists is easy. Knowing that a solution exists can tell you almost nothing about how many there are.

Toda’s theorem: $\mathbf{PH} \subseteq \mathbf{P}^{\#\mathbf{P}}$. Exact counting is at least as powerful as the entire polynomial hierarchy put together.

Interactive Proofs: IP = PSPACE

A classical proof is written down once and checked. An interactive proof is a conversation: an all-powerful prover and a randomized polynomial-time verifier exchange messages. The honest prover must win on yes-instances with probability at least 2/3 (completeness), and no prover can win on no-instances with probability above 1/3 (soundness).

Shamir (1992): IP = PSPACE. A short conversation can establish anything in PSPACE, including that a quantified Boolean formula is false. The technique, arithmetization, turns formulas into polynomials and has the verifier check them at random points: by the Schwartz–Zippel lemma, a lying prover gets caught with high probability. Polynomial Identity Testing explains the lemma.

A gentle example is graph non-isomorphism. The verifier secretly picks one of the two graphs, scrambles its vertex labels, and asks which one it was. If the graphs really differ, the prover can always tell. If they’re secretly the same, the prover can only guess, and is caught half the time. Ten rounds push a cheat’s chances below $2^{-10}$.

Zero-Knowledge Proofs

Zero-knowledge goes further: prove something is true without revealing why. Here’s a proof that you know a 3-coloring of a graph, which never shows the coloring:

Every round the prover renames the three colors at random and locks every vertex away; the verifier opens just one edge. A real coloring always shows two different colors. A fake has at least one bad edge, and each round has at least a $1/|E|$ chance of hitting it. One round isn’t much, but after $k \cdot |E|$ rounds a cheat survives with probability only about $e^{-k}$. Meanwhile each opened edge shows two different, freshly renamed colors, which says nothing about the real coloring.

With cryptographic commitments to lock the colors away, this works for every NP problem. Zero-knowledge proofs now underpin privacy-preserving authentication and many blockchain systems.

PPAD and Nash Equilibria

Some problems always have a solution, guaranteed by a mathematical theorem rather than an algorithm. Every finite game has a Nash equilibrium, by fixed-point theorems; finding one is another matter.

PPAD, “polynomial parity argument on directed graphs”, captures exactly these: total search problems whose solutions are guaranteed by a parity argument, in a graph where every unbalanced vertex has a partner at the other end of a path.

Computing a Nash equilibrium, even of a two-player game, is PPAD-complete (Chen and Deng, 2006, building on Daskalakis, Goldberg and Papadimitriou). PPAD is a class of search problems sitting between FP and FNP, the function versions of P and NP. It’s unlikely to be NP-hard, since a solution always exists, yet no efficient algorithm is expected. That’s worst-case hardness: it suggests no fast method finds equilibria for every game, which is why algorithms for markets and games lean on special structure.

“Does this zoo matter in practice?" Yes. #P-hardness explains why computing partition functions in statistical physics, or network reliability, is hard. PPAD-completeness explains why exact equilibria are elusive in big strategic settings. IP = PSPACE and zero-knowledge underpin real cryptographic protocols. Often the useful question isn’t “is it in NP?” but “which kind of hard is it?”

Complexity isn’t a single line from easy to hard. It’s a landscape, where different kinds of hardness come from different resources and kinds of proof. P vs NP is the most famous open question in it, but the landscape around it is already rich with answers.
