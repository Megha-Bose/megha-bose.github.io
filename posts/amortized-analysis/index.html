Amortized Analysis - The True Cost of a Sequence

Aug 2021
6 min read

Helpful context:

  • Data Structures - Storage Is Strategy

A dynamic array doubles its capacity when it fills up. Most appends just write a value into the next free slot. But every so often the array is full, and the append has to allocate a bigger block and copy all $n$ elements across: $O(n)$.

So is appending $O(n)$? For that one unlucky call, yes. But ask what $n$ appends cost in total, starting from empty, and the answer is $O(n)$: linear, not quadratic. The rare expensive steps are paid for by the many cheap ones.

That’s amortized analysis: instead of the worst single operation, bound the cost per operation over any sequence. It’s often dramatically lower, and it’s a guarantee, not an estimate.

Not average-case analysis. Average-case analysis assumes something about random inputs. Amortized analysis assumes nothing: it’s a worst-case bound on sequences. “Average” here means averaged over time, over the operations in one run, not averaged over possible inputs.

Amortization pays off when some operations are expensive, they’re rare, and the cheap ones before them “set them up”. The classic cases are dynamic arrays, hash tables that occasionally rehash, and trees that occasionally restructure. There are three ways to make the argument. They all reach the same bound; they just tell the story differently.

Method 1: The Aggregate Method

Add up the total cost $T(n)$ of any $n$ operations, and divide by $n$.

This is an interactive demo. It needs JavaScript to run.

Start with capacity 1 and append $n$ times. Every append writes once: $n$ in total. Resizes happen at sizes 1, 2, 4, 8, …, and each copies everything present:

$$1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots + 2^{\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor} < 2n$$

since each term is double the one before, so the sum is less than twice the largest term, which is at most $n$. Total: less than $n + 2n = 3n$. Per append: at most 3, so $O(1)$ amortized, even though one append can cost $O(n)$.

Method 2: The Accounting Method

Charge each operation a fixed fee. When an operation costs less than its fee, bank the difference as credit. When one costs more, pay from the bank. The single rule: the bank must never go negative. If a fee of $f$ always keeps the bank solvent, every operation is $O(f)$ amortized.

This is an interactive animation. It needs JavaScript to run.

For the dynamic array, charge 3 credits per append: 1 pays for writing the value, and 2 are stored on the new element. When an array of capacity $k$ is full, the $k/2$ elements added since the last resize carry 2 credits each, which is $k$ credits: exactly the cost of copying all $k$ elements. The bank never goes negative, so appends are $O(1)$ amortized.

This method makes the pre-payment explicit: every cheap operation saves just enough for its share of the next expensive one.

Method 3: The Potential Method

The most general method. Define a potential $\Phi$, a non-negative number computed from the data structure’s current state, like energy stored in a spring. It measures how much deferred work is waiting. The amortized cost of operation $i$ is its actual cost plus the change in potential:

$$\hat c_i = c_i + \Phi(D_i) - \Phi(D_{i-1})$$

Summed over a sequence, the potential terms telescope:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat c_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i + \Phi(D_n) - \Phi(D_0)$$

So as long as the potential ends at least where it started, the real total is at most the amortized total. The art is choosing $\Phi$ so that it rises a little on cheap operations and drops sharply on expensive ones, cancelling out their cost.

For the dynamic array, take $\Phi = 2 \cdot \text{size} - \text{capacity}$. Just after a resize it’s small, 2; just before the next, when the array is full at size $k$, it’s $k$.

  • Ordinary append: actual cost 1, size up by 1, so $\Phi$ rises by 2. Amortized: $1 + 2 = 3$.
  • Resizing append: actual cost $k + 1$ (copy $k$, write 1). $\Phi$ falls from $k$ to $2(k+1) - 2k = 2$, a change of $2 - k$. Amortized: $(k + 1) + (2 - k) = 3$.

Every append is exactly 3 amortized: the potential’s drop swallows the copying cost.

“Why not just call append $O(n)$ worst case?" Because charging every operation its worst case badly overcounts. That would put $n$ appends at $O(n^2)$, when we’ve just proved they cost $O(n)$. Dynamic arrays would look no better than linked lists, when in practice they’re far faster. Amortized analysis gives the accurate picture.

Application: Splay Trees

A splay tree is a binary search tree that stores no balance information at all. Every access rotates the accessed node all the way up to the root (“splaying”), so frequently used keys drift toward the top.

With the potential

$$\Phi(T) = \sum_{x \in T} \log(\text{size of the subtree rooted at } x)$$

splaying a node costs $O(\log n)$ amortized, so any $m$ operations on $n$ keys cost $O((m + n)\log n)$. Single operations can be slow, but the sequence is fast. And the tree adapts: access one key over and over, and it stays at the root, where it’s cheap to reach.

Application: Union-Find

Union-Find tracks a collection of disjoint sets with two operations: find the representative of $x$’s set, and union two sets. Two tricks make it fast:

  • Path compression: on every find, point each node on the path straight at the root.
  • Union by rank: attach the shallower tree under the deeper one.

Together they give $O(\alpha(n))$ amortized per operation, where $\alpha$ is the inverse Ackermann function. It grows so slowly that $\alpha(n) \leq 4$ for any $n$ smaller than the number of atoms in the observable universe: constant in every practical sense. The proof is one of the harder amortized analyses, but the intuition is simple: compression flattens the trees, so future finds get cheaper, and union by rank keeps the trees shallow enough that compressing never costs much. Minimum Spanning Trees & Union-Find puts it to work.

Summary

Method Core idea Use it when
Aggregate Total cost of $n$ operations, divided by $n$ You can count the total work directly
Accounting Fixed fees; cheap operations save for expensive ones There’s a natural pre-payment story
Potential Amortized = actual + change in stored “energy” $\Phi$ Complex structures; the most general

With the potential method, once you have the right $\Phi$ the algebra is mechanical. Finding $\Phi$ is the art, and a good rule of thumb is to make it large just before the expensive operations and small just after them.
