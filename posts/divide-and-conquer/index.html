Divide & Conquer - Small Savings That Compound

Aug 2021
8 min read

Helpful context:

  • Sorting Algorithms - How Order Turns Hard Problems Easy
  • Amortized Analysis - The True Cost of a Sequence

In 1969 Volker Strassen surprised the whole field of numerical computing. Everyone assumed multiplying two $n \times n$ matrices needed about $n^3$ scalar multiplications, three nested loops. Split each matrix into $2 \times 2$ blocks and the obvious method needs 8 block multiplications. Strassen found a way to do it with 7.

One fewer multiplication doesn’t sound like much. But the saving repeats at every level of the recursion, and compounds: the exponent drops from 3 to 2.807, and at $n = 10{,}000$ that’s about 6 times less work.

That’s divide and conquer. Splitting a problem in half and recursing is the easy part. The insight is that a small saving at each level of the recursion becomes a large saving overall.

The Template

  1. Divide the problem into $a$ subproblems, each of size about $n/b$.
  2. Conquer each one recursively, solving tiny ones directly.
  3. Combine their answers, at a cost of $O(n^c)$.

That gives the recurrence

$$T(n) = a \cdot T(n/b) + O(n^c)$$

and its solution depends only on where the work concentrates in the recursion tree. Pick an algorithm below, or set $a$, $b$ and $c$ yourself:

Each level does $r = a / b^c$ times the work of the level above, which gives the three cases of the Master Theorem :

  • $c < \log_b a$: the leaves dominate, $T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_b a})$.
  • $c = \log_b a$: every level is equal, $T(n) = \Theta(n^c \log n)$.
  • $c > \log_b a$: the root dominates, $T(n) = \Theta(n^c)$.

Merge sort splits into 2 halves and merges in $O(n)$: $a = b = 2$, $c = 1 = \log_2 2$. Every level does $O(n)$ work across $\log n$ levels: $\Theta(n \log n)$.

Binary search recurses into just 1 half with constant work: $a = 1$, $b = 2$, $c = 0 = \log_2 1$. Also the every-level-equal case: $\Theta(\log n)$. There’s no combine step at all.

Karatsuba Multiplication: Three Multiplies Instead of Four

Schoolbook multiplication of two $n$-digit numbers takes $O(n^2)$ digit products. Split each number in half, $x = x_1 B^{n/2} + x_0$ and $y = y_1 B^{n/2} + y_0$, where $B$ is the base:

$$xy = x_1 y_1 \cdot B^n + (x_1 y_0 + x_0 y_1) \cdot B^{n/2} + x_0 y_0$$

That looks like four half-size products. Karatsuba noticed the middle term can reuse the other two:

$$x_1 y_0 + x_0 y_1 = (x_1 + x_0)(y_1 + y_0) - x_1 y_1 - x_0 y_0$$

So three products suffice: $x_1 y_1$, $x_0 y_0$ and $(x_1 + x_0)(y_1 + y_0)$. With $a = 3$, $b = 2$, $c = 1$, and $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 > 1$, the leaves dominate:

$$T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_2 3}) \approx \Theta(n^{1.585})$$

For million-digit numbers, that’s around $10^{9.5}$ operations instead of $10^{12}$: about 30 times fewer.

A worked example: $12 \times 34$, splitting at the tens. Here $x_1 = 1$, $x_0 = 2$, $y_1 = 3$, $y_0 = 4$:

Product Value
$x_1 y_1 = 1 \times 3$ 3
$x_0 y_0 = 2 \times 4$ 8
$(x_1 + x_0)(y_1 + y_0) = 3 \times 7$ 21
Middle term: $21 - 3 - 8$ 10
Result: $3 \cdot 100 + 10 \cdot 10 + 8$ 408

Closest Pair of Points in $O(n \log n)$

Given $n$ points in the plane, find the two closest. Checking every pair is $O(n^2)$. Divide and conquer:

  1. Sort the points by $x$ once, in $O(n \log n)$.
  2. Split them at the median $x$ into a left half and a right half.
  3. Recursively find the closest pair in each half. Let $\delta$ be the smaller of the two distances.
  4. The overall closest pair might straddle the line. But such a pair must lie within $\delta$ of the line, so look only at the strip of width $2\delta$ around it. Sort the strip by $y$, and compare each point with just its next 7 neighbours.

Why only 7? Points on the same side are at least $\delta$ apart, and only 8 points that far apart fit in a $\delta \times 2\delta$ box. Keeping the points pre-sorted by $y$ makes each strip step $O(n)$, so $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n \log n)$.

Strassen’s Matrix Multiplication

Write each matrix as four $n/2 \times n/2$ blocks. Then each block of the product follows the ordinary rule, with blocks playing the role of numbers:

$$C_{11} = A_{11}B_{11} + A_{12}B_{21} \qquad C_{12} = A_{11}B_{12} + A_{12}B_{22}$$ $$C_{21} = A_{21}B_{11} + A_{22}B_{21} \qquad C_{22} = A_{21}B_{12} + A_{22}B_{22}$$

Why blocks can be multiplied like numbers

Each entry of $C = AB$ is a dot product, $C[i][j] = \sum_k A[i][k], B[k][j]$. Split that sum at the midpoint of $k$:

$$C[i][j] = \sum_{k < n/2} A[i][k], B[k][j] + \sum_{k \geq n/2} A[i][k], B[k][j]$$

For $i$ and $j$ in the top-left quadrant, the first half only touches $A_{11}$ and $B_{11}$, and the second only $A_{12}$ and $B_{21}$. That holds for every entry in the quadrant, so $C_{11} = A_{11}B_{11} + A_{12}B_{21}$. The other blocks follow the same way. It’s the scalar formula $C_{IJ} = \sum_K A_{IK} B_{KJ}$ with block indices.

You could split into $3 \times 3$ blocks instead, but the best known scheme there (Laderman, 1976) needs 23 of the 27 block products, giving $O(n^{\log_3 23}) \approx O(n^{2.854})$: worse than Strassen.

Done directly, that’s 8 block products: $T(n) = 8T(n/2) + O(n^2) = O(n^3)$, no gain. Strassen instead forms 7 products of sums and differences, then recombines them using additions only:

Expand $C_{11} = M_1 + M_4 - M_5 + M_7$ and almost everything cancels, leaving $A_{11}B_{11} + A_{12}B_{21}$. The other three blocks check out the same way. Now $T(n) = 7T(n/2) + O(n^2)$, and $\log_2 7 \approx 2.807 > 2$, so

$$T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_2 7}) \approx \Theta(n^{2.807})$$

Code: Strassen in Python 
def strassen(A, B):
    n = len(A)
    if n == 1:
        return [[A[0][0] * B[0][0]]]

    mid = n // 2

    def split(M):
        top = [row[:mid] for row in M[:mid]]
        right = [row[mid:] for row in M[:mid]]
        bot_left = [row[:mid] for row in M[mid:]]
        bot_right = [row[mid:] for row in M[mid:]]
        return top, right, bot_left, bot_right

    def add(X, Y):
        return [[X[i][j] + Y[i][j] for j in range(len(X[0]))] for i in range(len(X))]

    def sub(X, Y):
        return [[X[i][j] - Y[i][j] for j in range(len(X[0]))] for i in range(len(X))]

    def combine(C11, C12, C21, C22):
        top = [C11[i] + C12[i] for i in range(mid)]
        bot = [C21[i] + C22[i] for i in range(mid)]
        return top + bot

    A11, A12, A21, A22 = split(A)
    B11, B12, B21, B22 = split(B)

    M1 = strassen(add(A11, A22), add(B11, B22))
    M2 = strassen(add(A21, A22), B11)
    M3 = strassen(A11, sub(B12, B22))
    M4 = strassen(A22, sub(B21, B11))
    M5 = strassen(add(A11, A12), B22)
    M6 = strassen(sub(A21, A11), add(B11, B12))
    M7 = strassen(sub(A12, A22), add(B21, B22))

    C11 = add(sub(add(M1, M4), M5), M7)
    C12 = add(M3, M5)
    C21 = add(M2, M4)
    C22 = add(sub(add(M1, M3), M2), M6)

    return combine(C11, C12, C21, C22)

In practice, Strassen only pays off for matrices beyond roughly 500 to 1,000 on a side. Below that, a carefully written triple loop wins, because it maps perfectly onto two things the asymptotics ignore. SIMD instructions multiply 8 or 16 numbers at once, and cache blocking keeps the working tiles in the fastest memory. Strassen fights both: it allocates seven temporary matrices, streams the whole input through memory for additions before multiplying anything, and its recursion scatters memory accesses. Its subtractions also amplify rounding error, which matters in scientific computing. So libraries like the BLAS behind NumPy mostly use tuned triple loops.

Beyond Strassen: the race for ω, and how optimal is 7?

The smallest exponent $\omega$ with $O(n^\omega)$ matrix multiplication has been creeping down for decades: Coppersmith and Winograd reached about 2.376 in 1990, and refinements of their “laser method” brought it to about 2.371 by 2024. The constant factors are so astronomical that nobody implements these; they’re purely theoretical. Many believe $\omega = 2$, but it’s unproven.

For $2 \times 2$ blocks, Strassen’s 7 is optimal: Winograd proved in 1971 that 6 multiplications can’t work. Strassen found his products by treating the block entries as bilinear forms and looking for a decomposition into fewer rank-one terms. Searching for such decompositions is hard, and in 2022 DeepMind’s AlphaTensor used reinforcement learning to rediscover known schemes and find some new ones for small sizes.
