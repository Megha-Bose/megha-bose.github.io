P, NP, and the Hardest Problems - The Question That Keeps Theorists Up at Night

Aug 2021
8 min read

Helpful context:

  • Turing Machines - The Minimal Model of Everything a Computer Can Do
  • Decidability & The Halting Problem - Questions No Program Can Answer

P vs NP is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems chosen by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000, each worth a million dollars. Six are still unsolved, this one included. (I suspect whoever cracks it won’t be doing it for the money.)

Nearly every cryptographer believes P $\neq$ NP: that encrypted data is safe from fast decryption in principle, not just from the methods we happen to know. Nearly every algorithms researcher believes it too: that the hard problems on their desks won’t suddenly turn easy. Decades of effort by the sharpest people in mathematics have produced no proof either way.

From “Solvable” to “Solvable Fast”

Decidability asked which problems have any algorithm. Complexity theory asks which ones have a fast one, and “fast” has a precise meaning: polynomial time, $O(n^k)$ steps for some fixed $k$ on inputs of size $n$.

Why polynomial? It’s closed under composition: a polynomial algorithm calling a polynomial subroutine a polynomial number of times is still polynomial. And it’s model-independent: Turing machines, RAM machines and real computers all simulate each other with only polynomial overhead, so they agree on what’s polynomial.

P: Problems You Can Actually Solve

$$\mathbf{P} = \bigcup_{k \geq 0} \text{TIME}(n^k)$$

  • Sorting: $O(n \log n)$.
  • Shortest paths: $O(m \log n)$ with Dijkstra.
  • Primality: polynomial in the number of digits, by the AKS algorithm. That one was only proved in 2002, after decades of trying.
  • Linear programming: polynomial via the ellipsoid method.

NP: Problems Where You Can Check an Answer

NP stands for nondeterministic polynomial, not “not polynomial”. It’s the class of yes/no problems where a proposed solution, a certificate, can be verified in polynomial time.

$$w \in L \iff \exists\ c \text{ with } |c| \leq \text{poly}(|w|) \text{ such that } V(w, c) \text{ accepts}$$

The travelling salesman problem above is the classic: with 10 cities there are 181,440 distinct tours, with 30 more than there are grains of sand on Earth, and with 100 more than there are atoms in the observable universe. But checking that one specific tour comes in under budget $B$ is just addition.

Problem The question Certificate Checking it
3-SAT Can this formula, built from 3-literal clauses, be made true? A true/false value for each variable Plug them in: $O(n)$
3-coloring Can the graph be colored with 3 colors, no edge joining same-colored vertices? The coloring Check every edge: $O(m)$
Hamiltonian path Is there a path visiting every vertex exactly once? The path Check adjacency and uniqueness: $O(n)$
Clique Are there $k$ vertices all connected to each other? The $k$ vertices Check every pair: $O(k^2)$

For each one, finding a solution seems to need trying exponentially many candidates.

The name comes from the equivalent definition: NP is what a nondeterministic Turing machine can decide in polynomial time, a machine that can guess the certificate and then verify it. Real machines can’t guess; they have to search.

The Question

$\mathbf{P} \subseteq \mathbf{NP}$ is easy: if you can solve a problem fast, you can check an answer fast by just solving it again. The open question is whether the two are equal.

$$\mathbf{P} \stackrel{?}{=} \mathbf{NP}$$

  • If P = NP, anything checkable is findable. RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography would fall. Any theorem with a reasonably short proof could be proved automatically. Scheduling, packing, protein folding and drug design would become tractable.
  • If P $\neq$ NP, there really are problems that are easy to check and hard to solve. Encryption is safe, and hard problems stay hard. This is the world almost everyone believes we live in.

“Why not just try everything?" Because exponential is catastrophically worse than polynomial. At $n = 1{,}000$, an $n^3$ algorithm takes about $10^9$ steps, a second or so. Trying all $2^{1000} \approx 10^{301}$ assignments would take longer than the universe has existed even at a step per femtosecond, with every atom in the universe computing. P vs NP asks whether a fundamentally smarter method exists, not whether brute force is slow.

NP-Hard and NP-Complete

A problem $X$ is NP-hard if every problem in NP reduces to it in polynomial time: there’s a fast translation of any NP instance into an instance of $X$ with the same answer. So a fast algorithm for $X$ would give a fast algorithm for everything in NP. A problem is NP-complete if it’s NP-hard and in NP itself: the hardest problems in NP.

Why “reduces”? It sounds like making the problem easier, but it means offloading it. “A reduces to B” means a black box for B would solve A, so A is no harder than B. To prove $X$ is NP-hard, you reduce a known hard problem to $X$: if $X$ can do the hard problem’s job, $X$ is at least that hard.

In the early 1970s Cook and Karp showed something remarkable: thousands of problems from scheduling, graph theory, logic and biology are all NP-complete, linked by a web of reductions. Solve any one of them in polynomial time and you’ve solved all of them. They’re the same problem in different costumes:

  • 3-SAT: Boolean satisfiability with 3-literal clauses
  • TSP, as a decision problem: is there a tour within budget $B$?
  • Graph coloring with $k$ colors
  • Knapsack: reach a target value without exceeding the weight limit
  • Clique, independent set, vertex cover
  • Scheduling jobs onto machines within deadlines

How to Prove a Problem Is NP-Complete

  1. Show it’s in NP. Describe a certificate and a polynomial-time check. For TSP: the tour, and adding up its edges.
  2. Show it’s NP-hard. Take a problem already known to be NP-complete, usually 3-SAT or something structurally close, and give a polynomial-time reduction from it to yours.

The reduction is the creative part: you design gadgets, small pieces of your problem that imitate the variables and clauses of the other. NP-Completeness builds several.

The very first NP-complete problem had nothing to reduce from. In 1971 Stephen Cook proved SAT is NP-complete directly: the run of any polynomial-time verifier on any input can be written as one Boolean formula, which is satisfiable exactly when the verifier accepts. Once SAT was on the map, every later proof only needed a reduction.

NP-Hard Problems Beyond NP

NP-complete means NP-hard and in NP. Some NP-hard problems lie far beyond NP:

  • Optimization problems. NP contains only yes/no questions. “Is there a tour of length at most $B$?” is NP-complete; “what is the shortest tour?” is NP-hard but not a yes/no question, so it isn’t in NP at all. Most real problems are optimization problems, so they land here.
  • Quantified Boolean formulas. Allow both “there exists” and “for all” in a formula, and deciding whether it’s true becomes PSPACE-complete, believed strictly harder than NP. It’s the logic of games: “is there a move such that, for every reply, I can still win?”
  • Generalized chess and Go. Deciding who wins from an $n \times n$ position is EXPTIME-complete: provably harder than polynomial time, whether or not P = NP.
  • The halting problem. Undecidable, and still NP-hard, since every NP problem reduces to it. “NP-hard” is a floor, not a description.

The Landscape

P sits inside NP. NP-complete problems are where NP meets NP-hard, and NP-hard extends far beyond NP, out to undecidable problems like halting.

What NP-Hardness Means in Practice

Proving your problem NP-hard isn’t defeat; it’s information. Stop hunting for a fast exact, worst-case algorithm, and reach for one of these instead:

  • Approximation algorithms with a guaranteed bound. For vertex cover: pick any edge not yet covered, take both its endpoints, and repeat. It’s fast, and it’s never more than twice the optimum. Approximation Algorithms covers more.
  • Heuristics that work well on the inputs you actually see: local search , simulated annealing .
  • Special structure: sparse graphs, small treewidth, small parameters.
  • Exact search with strong pruning: branch and bound , integer programming solvers , SAT solvers . They’re exponential in the worst case and astonishingly good in practice.

Summary

Class Informal definition Examples
P Solvable in polynomial time Sorting, shortest paths, primality
NP Solutions checkable in polynomial time 3-SAT, TSP, coloring, clique
NP-hard At least as hard as everything in NP TSP (optimization), halting
NP-complete NP-hard and in NP 3-SAT, clique, vertex cover, knapsack

P is inside NP, and NP-complete problems are the hardest problems in NP. Whether P = NP is the central open question. The evidence points strongly to P $\neq$ NP, but nobody has a proof.
